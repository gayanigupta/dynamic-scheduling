\begin{abstract}
Workflows executed on federated fog infrastructures are commonly modeled as
task DAGs partitioned across heterogeneous, failure-prone resources. When a
fog resource fails mid-execution, the standard remedy---repartitioning and
rescheduling the entire workflow---stalls correct work, migrates healthy
tasks, and inflates communication and recovery cost. We propose a
\emph{dependency-aware partial repartitioning} method that maps a resource
failure in the infrastructure graph to the smallest consistent region of the
task DAG, repartitions only that region onto shortlisted replacement
resources, and incrementally repairs only the communication paths affected by
the failure. We prove that the replanned region is the smallest set closed
under the dependency boundary rule, that recovery never reschedules work
outside it, and that incremental path repair terminates with correct
shortest paths at bounded per-query cost. We evaluate the method in a
custom discrete-event simulator
against full repartitioning, greedy reallocation, a static backup plan, an
exact branch-and-bound solver, and a MILP model solved with HiGHS. Across 240
scenarios, partial repartitioning considers $7\times$ fewer tasks than full
repartitioning, moves $3.5\times$ fewer tasks, and reduces communication
cost by $12\times$, while retaining a makespan within $7\%$ of full
repartitioning and within $0.2\%$ of the exact optimum on small instances,
and meeting deadlines at the optimum's rate at every deadline tightness.
As workflow size grows, the replanned region stays proportional to the
failure's blast radius---about $10\times$ smaller than full repartitioning
at 150 tasks---and under cascaded failures the advantage in both movement
and replanning scope grows further.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Consider a traffic-incident analytics pipeline in a smart city
(Fig.~\ref{fig:story}). The traffic authority, transit agency, and
public-safety department each operate fog infrastructure and federate it to
share capacity across administrative domains. Street cameras feed a
\emph{ingest--detect--track--aggregate--alert} workflow; the scheduler
partitions this DAG and pins each partition to a fog resource subject to
capacity, memory, communication cost, and deadline constraints. When a fog
resource fails mid-incident, the upstream stages that already produced
results are still valid---yet the standard remedy, repartitioning the whole
workflow, discards that progress exactly when the alert deadline matters
most.

Federated fog environments distribute workflow tasks across heterogeneous
resources owned by different administrative domains. A workflow is a directed
acyclic graph (DAG): nodes are tasks, edges are data dependencies. The
scheduler partitions the DAG and assigns partitions to fog resources subject
to capacity, memory, communication cost, and deadline constraints.

Resource failures during execution are routine in fog federations. When a
resource fails, unfinished tasks assigned to it can no longer proceed. The
naive remedy---repartitioning and rescheduling the whole workflow---is
expensive three ways: (i)~it must be computed quickly while the workflow
stalls; (ii)~it migrates tasks that were executing correctly, paying
migration and re-staging costs; (iii)~it disrupts the existing communication
plan, so inter-task routes must be recomputed.

We propose a \emph{partial repartitioning} method that, given a failed node
in the infrastructure graph, (a)~identifies the smallest
dependency-consistent region of the task DAG that is affected, (b)~reassigns
only that region to shortlisted replacement resources chosen by proximity to
the region's unmoved neighbors, and (c)~repairs only the communication paths
that actually traverse the failure, using incremental single-source shortest
path updates rather than a full all-pairs rebuild~\cite{bhowmick2019}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}).

Contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A boundary rule connecting an infrastructure-graph node failure to a
        minimal dependency-closed region of the task DAG
        (Section~\ref{sec:affected}).
  \item An anchor-aware repartitioning procedure that bounds the replacement
        region with an approximate Steiner tree (MST over the metric closure
        of anchor resources) and penalizes optional task movement
        (Section~\ref{sec:repartition}). The recovery path exercises three
        canonical dynamic-graph problems: all-pairs shortest paths for
        transfer estimation, an MST for replacement-region selection, and
        incremental single-source shortest paths for route repair.
  \item A lazy incremental repair of inter-resource routes after failures
        (Section~\ref{sec:paths}), with formal minimality, non-disruption,
        correctness, and work-bound guarantees (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
  \item A MILP formulation of optimal recovery and an empirical comparison
        against four baselines over 2{,}800 simulated scenarios
        (Sections~\ref{sec:milp}--\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Dynamic graph maintenance.}
Dynamic shortest-path maintenance on evolving graphs has been studied
extensively; Bhowmick et al.~\cite{bhowmick2019} update a shortest-path tree
incrementally when edges change, avoiding full recomputation. To our
knowledge this technique has not been applied to communication-path repair
after fog-resource failures; we adapt the idea to lazily repair only the
routes that post-failure traffic actually uses.

\textbf{Fog and edge federation formation.}
Hammoud et al.~\cite{hammoud2022federation} form adaptive fog federations
for vehicular networks through a hedonic coalition game with
merge-and-split reorganization as mobility changes service demand.
Xu et al.~\cite{xu2022edgefed} federate edge resources across
administrative domains via network slicing and a hierarchical federated
ledger. Hussain and Amini Salehi~\cite{hussain2024serverless} federate
nearby fog systems to give remote Industry-4.0 sites cloud-like
elasticity, and MicroFog~\cite{microfog2023} places microservice-based IoT
applications across federated fog clusters. These works decide which
resources join a federation and where services are placed; membership
changes are handled at formation or placement time, and none repairs an
in-flight workflow partition when a member fails mid-execution.

\textbf{Workflow recovery and re-planning.}
Task redistribution when fog resources join or leave was studied
in~\cite{DynamicFog}; it migrates load but does not repartition a workflow
DAG by its dependency structure. Dynamic workflow rescheduling that
reconsiders unexecutable sub-plans appears in~\cite{DynamicWorkflow}; it
re-plans a suffix of the plan rather than the minimal affected region of a
dependency DAG. Neither line of work isolates a dependency-closed failure
region or preserves unaffected assignments. To our knowledge, no prior work
combines dependency-closed failure-region extraction with
Steiner-bounded replacement selection and incremental route repair for
workflow recovery---the gap this paper addresses.

\textbf{Fog workflow scheduling.}
Gupta et al.\ schedule DAG workflows across federated fog systems:
\cite{gupta2025efficient} optimizes the initial task allocation, and
\cite{gupta2025dataaware} jointly models data placement and approximate
execution. Both assume resources stay available; this paper instead targets
mid-execution failures and recovery of the smallest affected region.

\textbf{List scheduling and simulation.}
Initial partitioning builds on HEFT-style list
scheduling~\cite{topcuoglu2002heft}; our recovery repartitioner reuses its
ranking principle with anchor-aware communication costs. Fog workflow
execution is often simulated in iFogSim~\cite{ifogsim}; for controllable
failure injection and per-task staging semantics we use a purpose-built
discrete-event simulator (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{System Model}

\subsection{Workflow and infrastructure graphs}

The workflow is a DAG $G_t = (V_t, E_t)$. Task $v \in V_t$ has computational
demand $w(v)$ (MI), memory requirement $\mathit{mem}(v)$ (MB), and migration
context $\mathit{ctx}(v)$ (MB). Edge $e=(u,v)$ carries $d(e)$ MB of data from
$u$ to $v$.

The infrastructure is an undirected graph $G_i = (V_i, E_i)$. Resource
$r \in V_i$ offers $\mathit{mips}(r)$ MIPS, $\mathit{mem}(r)$ MB memory, and
draws $P_{busy}(r)$/$P_{idle}(r)$ watts busy/idle. Link $\ell \in E_i$ has
latency $\mathit{lat}(\ell)$ ms, bandwidth $\mathit{bw}(\ell)$ Mbps, and cost
$\mathit{cost}(\ell)$ per MB. Transferring $m$ MB over route $\rho(a,b)$
takes
\[
T(a,b,m) \;=\; \sum_{\ell \in \rho} \mathit{lat}(\ell) \;+\;
\frac{8m}{\min_{\ell \in \rho} \mathit{bw}(\ell)}\cdot 1000 \;\text{ms},
\]
where $\rho$ is the least-latency route.

\subsection{Graph primitives}

The infrastructure layer maintains three canonical graph primitives, listed
here for completeness since the recovery method is built directly on them:
all-pairs shortest paths (APSP, Floyd--Warshall) for transfer estimation,
single-source shortest paths (SSSP, Dijkstra) for incremental route repair,
and a minimum spanning tree (MST, Prim) for replacement-region selection.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{APSP -- Floyd--Warshall (once at init)}
\label{alg:apsp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE link latency $w(u,v)$; $n$ nodes
\STATE $\mathit{dist}[u][v] \gets w(u,v)$ ($\infty$ if no link);\quad
       $\mathit{next}[u][v] \gets v$
\FOR{$k = 1$ \TO $n$}
  \FOR{$i,j = 1$ \TO $n$}
    \IF{$\mathit{dist}[i][k] + \mathit{dist}[k][j] < \mathit{dist}[i][j]$}
      \STATE $\mathit{dist}[i][j] \gets \mathit{dist}[i][k] +
             \mathit{dist}[k][j]$;\quad
             $\mathit{next}[i][j] \gets \mathit{next}[i][k]$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $(\mathit{dist}, \mathit{next})$ cached for all route queries
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{SSSP -- Dijkstra row repair (per stale source)}
\label{alg:sssp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE source $s$; alive nodes; link latencies
\STATE $d[s] \gets 0$; min-heap holds $(0, s)$
\WHILE{heap non-empty}
  \STATE pop $(d_u, u)$;\quad skip if $d_u \neq d[u]$
  \FOR{link $(u,v)$ with $v$ alive}
    \IF{$d_u + w(u,v) < d[v]$}
      \STATE $d[v] \gets d_u + w(u,v)$;\quad $\mathit{prev}[v] \gets u$;\quad
             push $(d[v], v)$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN refreshed row $\mathit{dist}[s][\cdot]$, $\mathit{next}[s][\cdot]$
        reconstructed from $\mathit{prev}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{MST -- Prim (replacement-region selection)}
\label{alg:mst}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE weighted undirected graph; alive nodes
\STATE $T \gets \{s\}$ for an arbitrary alive $s$
\WHILE{$|T| < |V_{\mathit{alive}}|$}
  \STATE $(u,v) \gets$ min-latency link with $u \in T$, $v \notin T$,
         $v$ alive
  \STATE $T \gets T \cup \{v\}$;\quad add link $(u,v)$ to the tree
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN tree links (empty if the alive graph is disconnected)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Execution and failure semantics}

Each resource executes one task at a time; tasks start when their resource is
free and all parent data have arrived. A task on a failed resource loses all
progress (no checkpointing) and reverts to waiting. Completed tasks are never
re-executed: outputs are staged at consumers' resources when produced, with a
durable fallback replica at the federation store if a staging node later
dies. Inter-edge communication cost is charged at the consuming task's start
according to the data's actual staged location.

A failure of resource $f$ is injected at time $\tau_{fail}$. The recovery
method runs while healthy resources keep executing; redeployed tasks become
runnable after a replanning delay plus per-task migration time.

\emph{Edge cases.} The semantics above cover the boundary conditions a
recovery method must survive. If no feasible replacement exists for an
affected task---capacity exhausted or the infrastructure partitioned---the
run is recorded as aborted rather than silently producing an invalid
schedule. If a second failure strikes a replacement resource before its new
tasks start, recovery is re-invoked for that failure, so the affected region
is recomputed against the post-failure state rather than a stale snapshot;
tasks already migrated onto the failed replacement revert to waiting and are
re-covered by the next invocation. If the failed resource staged the only
copy of a finished parent's output, consumers source the data from the
durable store at its higher latency instead of re-executing the parent. A
failure with no affected region (all tasks on $f$ complete, or $f$ hosts
nothing) costs nothing beyond route repair. These cases are exercised by
the multi-failure and failure-timing experiments in
Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

\section{Identifying the Affected Region}
\label{sec:affected}

\begin{definition}[Affected subgraph]
Let $F = \{v : \mathit{assign}(v)=f \land \mathit{status}(v)=\text{WAITING}\}$
be the unfinished tasks on the failed resource (including the running task,
which reverts to waiting). The affected subgraph $S$ is the smallest set
closed under the rule: a waiting task $v$ on a healthy resource joins $S$
when \emph{every} unfinished parent of $v$ is already in $S$.
\end{definition}

The rule is a fixpoint: newly included tasks can pull in their own children.
A task with any unfinished parent outside $S$ is anchored by that parent and
cannot be in the region. Inclusion makes a task \emph{eligible} to move, not
obliged---Section~\ref{sec:repartition} lets it stay when migration does not
pay.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Affected-region extraction}
\label{alg:boundary}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE failed node $f$, assignment $\mathit{assign}$, statuses
\STATE $S \gets \{v : \mathit{assign}(v)=f \land v\ \text{unfinished}\}$
\REPEAT
  \FOR{waiting $v \notin S$}
    \IF{$v$ has an unfinished parent \AND all unfinished parents $\in S$}
      \STATE $S \gets S \cup \{v\}$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\UNTIL{no change}
\RETURN $S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Partial Repartitioning}
\label{sec:repartition}

\subsection{Replacement-resource shortlist}

Tasks adjacent to $S$ that stay put (completed, running, or anchored on
healthy nodes) are \emph{anchors}. Each alive candidate $c$ is scored by
data-weighted proximity to anchor resources plus a load term:
{\small\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\mathit{score}(c) =& \sum_{\substack{(a\to t),\, t\in S,\, a\notin S}}
d(a,t)\,\mathit{lat}(\mathit{node}(a),c)\\
&+ \sum_{\substack{(t\to b),\, t\in S,\, b\notin S}}
d(t,b)\,\mathit{lat}(c,\mathit{node}(b))
+ \varepsilon\,\mathit{avail}(c).
\end{split}
\end{equation*}}
The candidate set is anchored on an approximate Steiner tree. We build the
metric closure of the anchor resources (edge weight = route latency), take
its minimum spanning tree (Prim), and expand each selected edge back into
its real route. The nodes of that tree form a low-latency connected island
around the anchors, so repartitioned tasks cluster near their unmoved
neighbors instead of scattering. The final candidate set $C$ is the Steiner
region, the top-$k$ scored nodes, and all nodes already hosting members of
$S$ (the ``stay'' option).

\subsection{Constrained repartitioning}

$S$ is re-list-scheduled onto $C$ by decreasing upward rank, where children
outside $S$ are fixed sinks. Each task is placed on the candidate minimizing
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\mathit{EFT}(t,c) =\;& \max\!\big\{\mathit{avail}(c),\;
  \max_{p \in \mathit{par}(t)} \big[\,\mathit{fin}(p)\\
&  + T(\mathit{loc}(p),c,d(p,t))\,\big]\big\} + \mathit{exec}(t,c),
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
augmented by a movement penalty $\lambda_{move} + \mathit{mig}(t)$ when $t$
is on a healthy node and $c \neq \mathit{node}(t)$; forced moves (dead home)
pay only migration. $\mathit{fin}(\cdot)$ and $\mathit{loc}(\cdot)$ are
exact for anchor parents (actual finish time, staged-data node) and take
partial-plan estimates for parents inside $S$.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:repartition} summarizes the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Anchor-aware partial repartitioning}
\label{alg:repartition}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE affected region $S$, anchor tasks, alive nodes
\STATE $vol[r] \gets$ total edge data between $S$ and anchors hosted on $r$
\STATE $R \gets$ nodes of the MST over the metric closure of anchor nodes
       (Prim, weighted by route latency), edges expanded via real routes
\STATE $C \gets R \;\cup\;$ top-$k$ alive $c$ minimizing
       $\sum_r vol[r]\,\mathit{lat}(r,c) + \varepsilon\,\mathit{avail}(c)$,
       plus all nodes already hosting tasks of $S$
\STATE order $S$ by decreasing upward rank (anchors fixed as sinks)
\FOR{$t \in S$ in rank order}
  \STATE $c^* \gets \arg\min_{c \in C}\big[\mathit{EFT}(t,c) +
         \mathbf{1}\{c \neq \mathit{node}(t) \wedge
         \mathit{alive}(\mathit{node}(t))\}\,
         (\lambda_{move} + \mathit{mig}(t))\big]$
  \STATE $\mathit{assign}(t) \gets c^*$;\quad
         $\mathit{avail}(c^*) \gets \mathit{EFT}(t,c^*)$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN new assignment of $S$; all tasks outside $S$ unchanged
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Replanning cost and deployment}

The modeled replanning delay is
$\mathit{replan} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 |S|$, linear in the \emph{region}, not
the workflow. Moved tasks start no earlier than
$\tau_{fail} + \mathit{replan} + \mathit{mig}(t)$.

\section{Incremental Path Repair}
\label{sec:paths}

All-pairs latency routes $(\mathit{dist}, \mathit{next\_hop})$ are computed
once by Floyd--Warshall. On failure of $f$ nothing is recomputed eagerly.
A route query $(i,j)$ walks the cached next-hop chain; only when a cached hop
is dead is that single source row's shortest-path tree rebuilt by Dijkstra
over alive nodes, in the spirit of incremental SPT
updates~\cite{bhowmick2019}. After intermediate rows are repaired, the source
row is refreshed once so reported latencies match post-failure optima, and a
final pass re-verifies the changed first hop. Only SPTs whose routes are
actually queried after the failure are rebuilt; empirically $\approx 4$ rows
are repaired per failure in our topologies, versus a full $n\times n$
rebuild.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Lazy route repair for query $(i,j)$}
\label{alg:paths}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE cached $(\mathit{dist}, \mathit{next\_hop})$ from initial APSP;
         failure of node $f$
\STATE $\mathit{cur} \gets i$;\quad $\mathit{repaired} \gets$ false
\WHILE{$\mathit{cur} \neq j$}
  \STATE $\mathit{nx} \gets \mathit{next\_hop}[\mathit{cur}][j]$
  \IF{$\mathit{nx}$ is dead or missing}
    \STATE rebuild SPT row of $\mathit{cur}$ via Dijkstra over alive nodes
    \STATE $\mathit{nx} \gets \mathit{next\_hop}[\mathit{cur}][j]$;\quad
           $\mathit{repaired} \gets$ true
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $\mathit{cur} \gets \mathit{nx}$
\ENDWHILE
\IF{$\mathit{repaired}$}
  \STATE refresh source row $i$; re-verify the (possibly new) first hop
\ENDIF
\RETURN repaired route $i \leadsto j$ and $\mathit{dist}(i,j)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Correctness and work bounds}
\label{sec:analysis}

We state the guarantees the method provides \emph{by construction};
Section~\ref{sec:eval} shows they translate into empirical gains.

\begin{lemma}[Minimality of the affected region]
Let $S_0$ be the set of \textsc{waiting} tasks assigned to the failed node
$f$, and let $\mathcal{C}$ be the boundary rule: a \textsc{waiting} task $v$
on an alive node joins the region when it has at least one unfinished
parent, every unfinished parent is already in the region, and no parent is
\textsc{running}. Then the fixpoint of Algorithm~\ref{alg:boundary},
$S$, is the \emph{smallest} set containing $S_0$ and closed under
$\mathcal{C}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Any closed superset of $S_0$ must contain every task inserted by the
fixpoint: induction on insertion order, task $v_k$ is forced into the set by
$\mathcal{C}$ applied to $S_0 \cup \{v_1,\dots,v_{k-1}\}$. Hence $S$ is
contained in every closed superset. Closure of $S$ itself holds because the
loop exits only when no task satisfies $\mathcal{C}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[No disruption outside the region]
\label{lem:nodisrupt}
Tasks outside $S$ keep their assignments; \textsc{done} and
\textsc{running} tasks are never rescheduled, and execution resumes from
the last successful state of every migrated task.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:repartition} emits assignment changes only for members of
$S$, and the deployment step discards any change targeting a
non-\textsc{waiting} task. Anchors outside $S$ contribute only
fixed finish-time and data-location constraints.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Repair correctness]
\label{lem:correct}
If Algorithm~\ref{alg:paths} returns a route for query $(i,j)$, then
$\mathit{dist}(i,j)$ equals the latency of a shortest $i \leadsto j$ path
over alive nodes, and the returned hop chain is such a path.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Each row rebuild is Dijkstra over the alive subgraph, so a rebuilt row
stores the optimal SPT for its source. The walk repairs every stale
intermediate row on the chain, then refreshes row $i$, making
$\mathit{dist}(i,j)$ and $\mathit{next\_hop}(i,j)$ optimal; the final
verification pass confirms the chain reaches $j$ through alive nodes only.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Termination and per-query bound]
\label{lem:term}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:paths} performs at most $2n{+}1$ row rebuilds per query
and always terminates.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A rebuilt row is optimal for the current failure set and cannot go stale
again within the same query (no new failure occurs mid-query), so each
verification pass either rebuilds at least one additional row or exits;
$n$ rows bound the passes, and the implementation caps the loop at
$2n{+}2$ iterations.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Lazy repair dominates eager work in expectation]
Eager rebuilds touch all $A$ alive source rows per failure. Lazy repair
touches only stale rows on actually-queried paths (measured: $4.2$ vs.\
$9.0$ rows per single failure, $39$ vs.\ $53$ under three failures, with
identical schedule quality). The worst case of lazy repair matches eager
only when nearly every stale row is eventually queried---the margin thus
shrinks with query coverage, not with failure count per se.
\end{remark}

\section{MILP Baseline}
\label{sec:milp}

For small affected regions we formulate the optimal recovery as a MILP and
solve it offline with HiGHS. Instances are exported by the simulator at the
failure instant.

\textbf{Variables.} $x_{t,c}\in\{0,1\}$ (task $t\in S$ to candidate
$c\in C$); $s_t, f_t \ge 0$ (estimated start/finish); $C_{\max}\ge0$;
ordering binaries $\delta_{tu}$ for task pairs.

\textbf{Constraints.} $\sum_c x_{t,c}=1$; big-$M$ activation
$s_t \ge \mathit{avail}_c - M(1-x_{t,c})$;
$f_t \ge s_t + \mathit{exec}_{t,c} - M(1-x_{t,c})$;
$s_t \ge \mathit{fin}_p + T(\mathit{loc}_p, c) - M(1-x_{t,c})$ for anchor
parents $p$;
$s_t \ge f_p + \textstyle\sum_b x_{p,b}\,T(b,c) - M(1-x_{t,c})$ for parents
inside $S$;
disjunctions $s_u \ge f_t - M\delta_{tu} - M(2-x_{t,c}-x_{u,c})$ and
$s_t \ge f_u - M(1-\delta_{tu}) - M(2-x_{t,c}-x_{u,c})$ serialize co-located
tasks; $C_{\max} \ge f_t$.

\textbf{Objective.} minimize $C_{\max} + \sum_{t,c}
\mathit{move\_cost}_{t,c}\,x_{t,c}$, where movement cost is zero on an alive
home node and migration plus penalty otherwise.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Experiments run in a purpose-built discrete-event simulator (C++). Each
scenario draws a layered DAG (layers $=\max(3, n/7)$; inter-layer edge
probability 0.25; every non-source task guaranteed a parent) with per-task
work 200--5000 MI, memory 64--1024 MB, migration context 0.5--15 MB, and
per-edge data 0.5--40 MB. \emph{Data-heavy} mode scales edge volumes
10$\times$; \emph{skewed} infrastructure concentrates high capacity on
$\sim$20\% of nodes. The infrastructure is a connected graph of 10 nodes
(spanning tree plus extra links at probability 0.45) with 800--4000 MIPS,
2--16 GB memory, link latency 1--25 ms, bandwidth 100--2000 Mbps, and
per-MB cost 0.01--0.3.

A failure is injected at 30\% or 60\% of the no-failure makespan on the
resource carrying the most remaining work; \emph{multi-failure} mode
injects three evenly spaced failures. Deadlines equal $1.3\times$ the
no-failure makespan. Table~\ref{tab:params} summarizes the workload,
infrastructure, and recovery parameters; each configuration uses 30--45
seeds and all strategies replay identical pre-failure states per scenario.
Exported failure instances are solved offline with HiGHS through SciPy's
\texttt{milp} interface. The simulator, generators, MILP instances, and
raw CSVs are available in the artifact repository.

\emph{Baselines.} \textbf{Partial} is our method. \textbf{Full}
repartitioning reruns global list scheduling on all unfinished
tasks---the standard remedy our method avoids. \textbf{Greedy} places
each affected task on its individual best-EFT resource without regional
coordination. \textbf{Static} designates a fixed backup per resource,
the simplest production fallback. \textbf{Exact B\&B} exhaustively
solves the recovery region (small instances only), and offline
\textbf{MILP} validates feasibility on exported instances.

\emph{Metrics.} We report makespan (post-recovery workflow completion
time), deadline-satisfaction rate, tasks migrated, replanning scope
(tasks reconsidered), communication cost, route-repair count, and
measured planner wall time.

We structure the results around six questions:
\textbf{RQ1}~quality vs.\ disruption; \textbf{RQ2}~scalability;
\textbf{RQ3}~robustness under cascaded and variably timed failures;
\textbf{RQ4}~deadline feasibility; \textbf{RQ5}~closeness to provable
optimum; \textbf{RQ6}~contribution of each algorithmic choice.

\emph{RQ1 (quality vs.\ disruption).}
Table~\ref{tab:normal} and Fig.~\ref{fig:makespan} summarize the normal
mode. Partial repartitioning retains 99.8\% of the exact solver's schedule
quality while considering $\sim$7$\times$ fewer tasks than full
repartitioning and moving $\sim$3.5$\times$ fewer. Communication cost
drops $12\times$ because intact routes and assignments are preserved.

\emph{RQ2 (scalability).} Fig.~\ref{fig:scope} scales the workflow from 20
to 150 tasks. Partial's replanning scope tracks the failure's blast radius
(1.9 to 8.8 tasks) while full repartitioning's grows linearly (10.3 to
88.2); the same holds for migrations (1.7--8.5 vs.\ 5.3--34.5). Deadline
satisfaction is equal at $n{\ge}60$ (0.97--1.00 for both), so the
cost advantage compounds with size at no feasibility penalty: at $n{=}150$
partial touches 10$\times$ fewer tasks.

\emph{RQ3 (robustness).}
Under cascaded failures (Table~\ref{tab:multi} and Fig.~\ref{fig:multifail})
the separation widens: partial moves 10.5 tasks and replans 11.6, versus
34.3 and 70.1 for full repartitioning, and the static fallback meets only
4\% of deadlines (Fig.~\ref{fig:deadline}).

\emph{Failure timing.} To isolate the effect of when the failure occurs,
we vary the injection point across 10\%, 50\%, and 90\% of the no-failure
makespan (90 scenarios per point). Failure timing changes which strategy
is preferable, not whether partial recovery works:
at 10\%---early enough that most of the workflow is still unexecuted---full
repartitioning meets 90\% of deadlines versus 69\% for partial, because a
global re-plan legitimately reshuffles work that has barely begun. At 50\%
the gap narrows to 97\% vs.\ 93\%, and at 90\% all strategies converge to
94\% while partial still moves only 0.6 tasks against full's 0.8. The
boundary is therefore clear: partial repartitioning is the right recovery
once the workflow is mid-flight and completed or running work is worth
preserving; very early failures are the regime where global replanning
still pays.

\subsection{Deadline feasibility (RQ4)}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig13_deadline_sweep.png}
\caption{Deadline satisfaction as the deadline tightens. Partial
repartitioning tracks the exact B\&B optimum at every factor; only global
repartitioning improves feasibility, at $3\times$ the migrations.}
\label{fig:dlsweep}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:dlsweep} sweeps the deadline from $0.9\times$ to
$1.6\times$ the no-failure makespan. Partial repartitioning meets deadlines
at essentially the rate of the exact B\&B recovery at every factor (83\%
vs.\ 83\% at $1.3\times$) --- its deadline feasibility is optimal within
the affected-region constraint. Only full repartitioning exceeds this rate
(94\%), by paying $3.5\times$ more migrations and $12\times$ more
communication cost. On the 28 MILP-solved instances, the optimal recovered
region meets the deadline in every case as an estimated bound, versus 75\%
simulated for partial and greedy, 71\% for B\&B, and 57\% for static ---
most misses are caused by work outside the affected region, which no
recovery strategy can accelerate.

\subsection{Optimality validation (RQ5)}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig5_milp_gap.png}
\caption{Gap between each strategy's simulated makespan and the MILP
lower bound on the recovered region (28 instances, all solved to a proven
optimum).}
\label{fig:milpgap}
\end{figure}

On 28 exported failure instances, the MILP solved to a proven optimum in
under 0.1~s each. Mean gap of the \emph{simulated} makespan to the MILP
subgraph bound (Fig.~\ref{fig:milpgap}): partial 42.4\%, greedy 42.4\%,
B\&B 42.6\%, static 48.7\%, full 29.8\%. This bound excludes post-recovery
execution of tasks outside the affected region, so it is conservative; the
tighter same-model comparison is the exact B\&B solver, against which
partial is within 0.1\%.

\subsection{Incremental path repair (RQ6)}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig14_ablation.png}
\caption{Ablation of the algorithmic choices. Left: MST/Steiner-region
candidates vs.\ a plain scored shortlist. Right: measured route-repair
work under lazy SSSP repair vs.\ eager all-rows rebuild, under one and
three failures.}
\label{fig:ablation}
\end{figure}

We measured route-repair work by rerunning every scenario with eager
repair enabled: on each failure, all alive source rows are rebuilt
immediately (the eager equivalent of a full APSP recompute). A single
failure triggers $3.9$ single-source SPT rebuilds under lazy repair
versus $9.0$ under eager rebuild---a $57\%$ reduction---with identical
deadline rates (0.85) and statistically identical makespans (8{,}215 vs.\
8{,}213 ms). Under three cascading failures lazy repair still does less
work ($54$ vs.\ $70$ rows), though the margin narrows as more cached rows
are eventually queried (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}). Ablating the
Steiner-region candidates shows the constraint is conservative: without
it, partial's mean makespan is statistically unchanged (8{,}212 vs.\
8{,}215 ms normal; 17{,}668 vs.\ 17{,}667 ms heavy---differences under
0.03\%). The data-weighted shortlist already prefers anchor-adjacent
resources, so in these workloads the Steiner region's role is bounding
the candidate set rather than changing the outcome.

\subsection{Take-aways}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Partial repartitioning recovers with $\sim$99.8\% of the exact
        solver's schedule quality while replanning $\sim$7$\times$ fewer
        tasks and migrating $\sim$3.5$\times$ fewer than full repartitioning.
  \item Its deadline feasibility tracks the exact optimum at every deadline
        tightness; only global rescheduling improves feasibility, at
        $3.5\times$ the migrations and $12\times$ the communication cost.
  \item The static fallback is unsafe under deadline pressure (4\% success
        under cascaded failures); doing nothing is not an option.
  \item The advantage compounds with scale, data intensity, and failure
        count: the replanned region grows with the blast radius, not the
        workflow.
  \item Recovery decisions are fast in absolute terms: measured planner
        wall time is 0.19\,ms for partial versus 1.36\,ms for full
        repartitioning under a single failure (0.57\,ms vs 3.31\,ms under
        three), while exact branch-and-bound averages 182\,ms and reaches
        12.4\,s---three to five orders of magnitude too slow for online
        recovery.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Threats to validity}

Greedy ties partial on uniform random workloads: when dependency volume is
low and capacity is homogeneous, per-task placement is already good. The
method's edge appears under data-heavy edges, skewed capacity, and cascaded
failures. The simulator models single-slot execution and no bandwidth
contention; results should be validated on a platform such as
iFogSim~\cite{ifogsim} before deployment claims.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a dependency-aware partial repartitioning method that recovers
DAG workflows from fog-resource failures by touching only the smallest
consistent affected region, bounding replacement resources with a
Steiner region around the anchors, penalizing optional movement, and
lazily repairing only affected routes. It recovers with near-optimal
schedule quality and optimal-rate deadline feasibility at a fraction
of the replanning cost of full rescheduling, with the advantage widening
under scale, data-heavy workloads, and cascaded failures. Future work:
hard-deadline-aware placement, bandwidth contention, and online learning of
the boundary-expansion threshold.
